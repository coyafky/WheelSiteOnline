    01Fitment6 min read

    How to measure your fitment

    The five numbers a forged wheel order actually needs, how to read them off a vehicle, and why a tape measure alone will get you into trouble.

    Read guide

    Expand for the short version, or open the full guide for the working detail.

    The five numbers that matter

    A forged wheel order is specified by five values: diameter, width, offset, PCD (bolt pattern) and centre bore. Everything else — face profile, lip depth, spoke concavity — follows from how those five interact with the vehicle.

    Diameter and width are straightforward and are the numbers customers quote. Offset is where most orders go wrong, because it is invisible to anyone who has not measured it and it changes how the wheel sits relative to the arch, the suspension and the brake caliper.

    Measuring offset without a wheel off the car

    Offset (ET) is the distance from the wheel's mounting face to its centreline, in millimetres. Positive means the mounting face is outboard of the centreline; negative means inboard.

    The reliable route for a vehicle that is new to you is to start from the OEM specification for that exact trim, then adjust deliberately and in small steps. If you are measuring a physical wheel, backspacing (the distance from the mounting face to the inboard edge) is easier to measure directly and converts back to offset once you know the width.

    What to send the factory

    Photograph the vehicle with the arch visible, note any brake or suspension modification, and give us the OEM specification you are starting from along with the stance you are targeting. We will check clearance and load rating against it and flag anything that will not fit before material is cut.

    02Fitment5 min read

    Understanding offset and backspacing

    Two ways of describing the same measurement, and the conversion that stops a customer's wheel from sitting 15mm further out than they expected.

    Read guide

    Expand for the short version, or open the full guide for the working detail.

    They describe the same thing from opposite ends

    Offset is measured from the wheel centreline. Backspacing is measured from the mounting face to the inboard lip. One is wheel-centric, the other is car-centric, and confusion between them is a common reason a quoted fitment comes out wrong.

    For a given width, the two are directly related — which means converting between them requires knowing the width, and a specification quoting backspacing without a width is incomplete.

    Why it matters at the arch

    On a wide-body or lowered car, the constraint is usually not the outboard arch but the inboard clearance: the inner lip against the suspension strut, control arm or brake caliper. That is a backspacing question, and it is the one a wheel that 'looks right' can still fail.

    Aggressive negative offsets push the wheel outboard and reduce inboard clearance. On a car with big brakes, that clearance is already tight before you change anything.

    Practical approach

    Agree the target stance with the customer first. Then we work back to an offset that delivers it without sacrificing brake or suspension clearance, and give you the resulting specification in writing before the order is cut.

    03Construction6 min read

    Monoblock vs two-piece: choosing a construction

    Lightest versus widest, stiffest versus serviceable. A decision framework rather than a sales pitch — including when two-piece is the wrong answer.

    Read guide

    Expand for the short version, or open the full guide for the working detail.

    Start from the constraint, not the look

    Monoblock wheels are cut from one forged blank. There is no join and no bolted centre, which makes them the lightest and stiffest construction we build and gives the tightest control on runout and balance.

    Two-piece wheels pair a forged centre with a separately formed barrel. That allows lip depth and step styling to be set independently of the spoke design, which is what produces the deep-lip look and the extreme widths a single piece cannot reach.

    When two-piece is the wrong answer

    If the customer is chasing unsprung weight and steering response, two-piece adds a joint and hardware that monoblock does not have. The correct answer is monoblock, and selling the deep lip instead because it looks better in a photograph is not engineering.

    Conversely, if the requirement is a 13-inch-wide rear on a wide-body build, monoblock may not be able to deliver it at all. The construction follows the requirement.

    Serviceability

    Two-piece barrels can be replaced without scrapping the forged centre. On a car that is regularly tracked or kerbed, that is a genuine economic argument over the life of the wheel — though it is not a reason to choose two-piece on its own.

    04Construction5 min read

    Forged vs cast wheels

    Why forging costs more, what the material actually does differently, and where a cast wheel is still the honest recommendation.

    Read guide

    Expand for the short version, or open the full guide for the working detail.

    What forging does to the material

    Forging works a solid billet under high pressure, which aligns the material's grain structure along the shape of the part. A casting is poured into a mould, so the grain structure is less directional and the material generally carries more porosity.

    The practical result is that a forged wheel can be both lighter and stronger than a cast wheel of the same dimensions — the material is doing more work per gram because it has been formed rather than poured.

    Where it shows up

    On a performance car the benefit is unsprung weight and stiffness, both of which the driver feels through steering response and ride. On a truck or SUV the benefit is load capacity and resistance to impact damage.

    PLACEHOLDER: any specific strength, weight-saving or load-rating figures quoted to a customer must come from Fenghuai's engineering team with a stated test basis.

    When cast is the honest answer

    If the budget does not support a forged wheel, a cast wheel sold as a cast wheel is a better outcome than a forged wheel sold on numbers that do not hold up. Say so and keep the customer.

    05Finishing5 min read

    Finishing options explained

    Brushed, polished, satin, matte and gloss — what each finish is, how it ages, and how to avoid a colour mismatch between samples and a delivered order.

    Read guide

    Expand for the short version, or open the full guide for the working detail.

    Surface versus colour

    Finishes have two independent attributes that customers often conflate. Surface texture — brushed, polished, satin, matte, gloss — describes how the metal interacts with light. Colour describes its hue.

    You can have a matte bronze and a gloss bronze; they are the same colour with different surface behaviour. Getting this distinction clear early prevents most finish disputes.

    Why swatches lie

    A flat colour swatch cannot show how linear grain catches light as a wheel turns, how a gloss behaves in direct sunlight, or how a finish reads against a specific body colour. Screen calibration changes it again.

    For anything other than a straightforward gloss or matte black, ask for a physical sample or a photograph of the exact finish on an actual wheel before committing a full order.

    Repeatability

    The real risk with finishes is not the first order — it is the second one. Repeatability depends on controlled process stages and on keeping a record of what was actually supplied. Ask what finish the previous order was, not what it was called.

    06Logistics7 min read

    Importing wheels: freight and duties

    Incoterms, HS codes, duty and the paperwork trail — a dealer's checklist for getting wheels across a border without a customs hold.

    Read guide

    Expand for the short version, or open the full guide for the working detail.

    Agree the incoterm before you price the job

    FOB, CIF and DDP are not interchangeable labels — they move who is responsible for freight, insurance, duty and clearance, and therefore move the landed cost materially. Quoting a customer before the incoterm is agreed is how margin disappears.

    PLACEHOLDER: confirm which incoterms Fenghuai actually offers and which it will not, and produce a one-page explanation for dealers.

    Classification and duty

    Wheels need a correct HS classification and a stated country of origin on the commercial invoice. Get the description wrong and the consignment stops at the border while it is resolved — usually at the worst possible moment for the customer.

    Duty and tax rates vary by destination and by classification. They must be calculated for the destination market rather than assumed from a previous shipment.

    Packing and damage claims

    Wheels are heavy and awkward, and once they are on a pallet they are handled by machinery rather than people. Freight-grade packing with reinforced corners is cheaper than a damage claim that takes eight weeks to resolve.

    Photograph the consignment before it leaves and on arrival, and keep the inspection record with the shipment documentation — it is what a claim is decided on.
